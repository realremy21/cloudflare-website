What the inspection covers

When we come out, we document:

  • Panel condition. Cracked, chipped, or delaminating glass; discoloration; anything on the glass that shouldn't be there. We photograph each panel so you can see what we saw.
  • Mounting and racking. How the array is attached, whether anything has loosened or shifted, and the condition of the roof where the system meets it.
  • Wiring visible from the roof. Conduit, cable runs, and connections we can see without opening anything up. Chewed insulation, sagging runs, and UV damage show up here — and a string can be offline from wire damage while the system looks perfectly fine from the ground.
  • Critter and insect signs. Nesting, droppings, debris packed under the array, and the entry points animals are using. This is where many inspections turn into a conversation about critter guards — pigeons and squirrels do more quiet damage to Denver systems than most owners realize.
  • Shading changes. Trees grow and neighbors build. A panel that produced fine three years ago can be half-shaded by a new branch, and we note what's changed since your system went in.
  • Soiling and production context. How dirty the panels are, and whether what you're seeing lines up with what the system should be producing. If the numbers don't add up, we say so — and we say what the next step is.

Then comes the part that makes it an inspection instead of a look: photos, a written report with itemized findings, and a repair estimate when something needs fixing. The report is written to be handed to someone else — an insurer, an adjuster, a buyer, your records — not just talked about on the roof.

What it does NOT include — said plainly

Our $245 inspection is a visual inspection. It does not include electrical testing.

If the visual inspection points to an electrical problem — an inverter error, a string that isn't producing, a reading that doesn't make sense — that's a separate step: our Electrical Diagnostic, $295, which is on-site electrical testing by our electrician, Dilan, with its own written report. Any repair work after that is quoted separately and approved by you before anything begins. Nobody starts work on your roof that you didn't agree to first.

We separate the two on purpose. Plenty of systems need only the visual inspection and a straight answer; combining everything into one vague "full inspection" price is how owners end up paying for testing they never needed — or believing they got testing they never received.

When a Denver inspection is worth it

  • After a hailstorm or heavy wind. Even a storm that didn't break glass can loosen mounts and shift hardware. Our storm documentation goes straight into your insurance claim file if you need it — photos and findings, dated and itemized.
  • Your bill or production changed and nobody knows why. A panel offline, a string down, new shading — an inspection finds the physical cause instead of guessing at it.
  • You just bought the house, or your installer disappeared. If nobody has looked at the roof in years — or the company that installed your system is gone — an inspection is how you find out what you actually own.
  • Before you spend money on the system. A guard install, a cleaning plan, a monitoring upgrade — inspecting first means you spend on the right thing, not the loudest thing.
  • It's been a year. Colorado weather is hard on rooftops: hail season, freeze-thaw winters, high-altitude sun. An annual look is how small problems stay small. Denver inspection questions

How much does a solar panel inspection cost in Denver?

Our Inspection & Report is $245 flat — the visual inspection, photographs of every panel, a written report with itemized findings, and a repair estimate when something needs fixing. The price is confirmed before we schedule. If electrical testing is needed, that's our separate Electrical Diagnostic at $295, quoted and approved by you first.

Is a "free inspection" the same thing?

Usually not. A free visit is typically paid for by whatever it sells at the end. Ask any company offering one: do I get photos, a written report with itemized findings, a repair estimate — and does it include electrical testing, or is it visual only? Either answer is fine; not knowing is the red flag. Our full breakdown of the difference is in our inspection guide on the blog.

Can the inspection report be used for an insurance claim?

Yes — that's one of the main reasons it's built the way it is. Photos, dated and itemized findings, and a repair estimate are exactly what adjusters work from, and our storm documentation is routinely handed straight into claim files.

Do you inspect systems your company didn't install?

Most of our inspections are exactly that — systems installed by someone else, including installers who are no longer in business. Knowing what's actually on your roof doesn't depend on who put it there.
