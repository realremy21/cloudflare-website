What a Real Solar Panel Inspection Covers (and What Most "Free Inspections" Skip)

By Jeremy & MeralPublished November 2, 2026

Your panels are up on the roof doing their job, and from the ground everything looks fine. That's the problem with solar: the things that actually go wrong — a chewed wire, a loosened mount, a nest packed under the array, a panel quietly underperforming — are all invisible from the driveway.

We're Jeremy and Meral Runnels, the husband-and-wife team behind Mile High Solar Care here in Aurora, and inspections are one of the most common reasons people call us across the Denver metro. Sometimes it's after a hailstorm. Sometimes it's because the electric bill crept up and nobody can say why. Sometimes it's because a system changed hands — a new homeowner, an installer that vanished — and nobody has looked at the roof in years.

Here's what a real inspection covers, what it costs with us, and how to tell whether the "free inspection" someone offered you is an inspection at all.

What we check on a real inspection

Our Inspection & Report is a thorough visual inspection of your solar array. When we come out, we document:

Panel condition. Cracked, chipped, or delaminating glass; discoloration; anything on the glass that shouldn't be there. We photograph each panel so you can see what we saw.

Mounting and racking. How the array is attached, whether anything has loosened or shifted, and whether the roof around the array shows any sign of trouble where the system meets it.

Wiring visible from the roof. Conduit, cable runs, and connections we can see without opening anything up. Chewed insulation, sagging runs, and UV damage all show up here — and a string can be offline from wire damage while the system looks perfectly fine from the ground.

Critter and insect signs. Nesting, droppings, debris packed under the array, and the entry points animals are using. This is where most of our inspections turn into a conversation about critter guards — pigeons and squirrels do more quiet damage to Denver systems than most owners realize. (We wrote the full guide to that problem here: Pigeon-Proofing Solar Panels: The Front Range Guide.)

Shading changes. Trees grow, neighbors build, and a panel that produced fine three years ago can be half-shaded by a new branch. We note what's changed since the system went in.

Soiling and production context. How dirty the panels are, and whether what you're seeing lines up with what the system should be producing. If the numbers don't add up, we say so — and we say what the next step is.

Then you get the part that makes it an inspection instead of a look: photos, a written report with itemized findings, and a repair estimate when something needs fixing. That report is yours. It's the same documentation people use for insurance claims, pest-control reimbursement, and storm records — because it's written to be handed to someone else, not just talked about on the roof.

What a real inspection does NOT include

This matters, so we'll say it plainly: our $245 inspection is a visual inspection. It does not include electrical testing.

If something in the visual inspection points to an electrical problem — an inverter error, a string that isn't producing, a reading that doesn't make sense — that's a separate step: our Electrical Diagnostic, which is on-site electrical testing by our electrician, Dilan, with its own written report. Any repair work after that is quoted separately and approved by you before anything begins. Nobody starts work on your roof that you didn't agree to first.

If another company offers you an "inspection" and can't tell you whether it includes electrical testing, photos, or a written report, that's your first red flag.

How to tell a real inspection from a sales visit

"Free inspection" is one of the most common offers in solar, and sometimes it's genuine. But a free visit is usually paid for by whatever it sells at the end of it. Before you book one, ask these five questions:

  1. Do I get photos? A real inspection documents what it found. If there's nothing to look at afterward, there was nothing to inspect.
  2. Do I get a written report with itemized findings? Not a verbal "looks good" and not a single-page quote. A report you can keep, hand to your insurer, or compare against another opinion.
  3. Is there a repair estimate, or just a sales proposal? An inspection tells you what's wrong and what fixing it costs — including the option that nothing needs fixing.
  4. Does it include electrical testing, or is it visual only? Either answer is fine. Not knowing the answer is not.
  5. Who's doing it, and what happens if they find something they can't fix? We tell you straight what we can handle, what needs an electrician, and what needs your installer or manufacturer. A company that only ever finds problems it happens to sell the solution for is not inspecting — it's prospecting.

If the answers to those questions are vague, the inspection is free because the pitch is the product.

When an inspection is worth it

You don't need an inspection every month. You do need one when:

  • A storm just hit. Hail and heavy wind are the obvious ones — but even a storm that didn't break glass can loosen mounts and shift things. Our storm documentation is built for exactly this, and the report goes straight into your claim file if you need it.
  • Your bill or production changed and nobody knows why. A panel offline, a string down, new shading — an inspection finds the physical cause instead of guessing at it.
  • You just bought the house, or your installer disappeared. If nobody has looked at the roof in years, or the company that installed it is gone, an inspection is how you find out what you actually own. (If your installer vanished, we wrote a whole guide for that situation: Your Solar Installer Went Bankrupt — Now What?)
  • You're about to spend money on the system. A guard install, a cleaning contract, a monitoring upgrade — an inspection first means you're spending on the right thing, not the loudest thing.
  • It's been a year. Colorado weather is hard on rooftops. An annual look — ideally paired with a cleaning — is how small problems stay small.

The short version

A real solar panel inspection in Denver gives you three things: photos of what was actually found, a written report with itemized findings, and a repair estimate when something needs fixing. If a visit doesn't leave you with those, it wasn't an inspection — it was a pitch with a ladder.
