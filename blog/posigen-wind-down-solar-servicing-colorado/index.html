Another Solar Company Is Gone — and This Time, the Servicing Itself Is in Dispute

By Jeremy & MeralPublished November 28, 2026

If you've read our earlier guides, you know the pattern by now. Freedom Forever went through bankruptcy. Mosaic's loans moved to a new servicer. Apex Solar Solutions left Colorado entirely. We wrote our full guide — Your Solar Installer Went Bankrupt — Now What? — because Colorado homeowners kept needing the same answers.

This month brought the next chapter, and it has a twist the earlier ones didn't: PosiGen.

PosiGen was a national residential solar company built on leases and power purchase agreements (PPAs), serving mostly low- and moderate-income households. Its Chapter 11 case ended in a liquidating plan that took effect on February 26, 2026, and Brookfield acquired substantially all of its assets through the bankruptcy sale. Then, on November 10, 2026, the plan administrator sued three of PosiGen's former officers and six former directors. The complaint alleges — and these are allegations in a court filing, not findings — that project-company cash was diverted to operating expenses, and that the same solar projects were sold or pledged to more than one counterparty.

Why should a Colorado homeowner care about a court fight in a Texas bankruptcy case? Because of what it says about the paperwork behind thousands of rooftops: when a company's projects may have been sold or pledged more than once, the question "who actually services my system?" stops being a formality. It becomes the first thing to verify — before anyone quotes you a repair, sells you a plan, or touches a panel.

What's genuinely different about the PosiGen wind-down

Most PosiGen customers never owned their systems. PosiGen's model was the lease and the PPA: the company (or a project company behind it) owned the panels, and the homeowner bought the power. That's the opposite of a cash purchase or a solar loan — and it changes what an orphaned owner can and can't do. If your system is leased or under a PPA, the system owner named in your agreement — or whoever lawfully took over that role — is generally the party responsible for service, and the party whose permission matters before outside work happens.

The servicing chain itself is what's being litigated. In the Freedom Forever and Mosaic cases, the homeowner's job was to find the new servicer and keep paying the right company. Here, the plan administrator's complaint alleges the underlying projects changed hands or were pledged in overlapping ways. Homeowners don't need to follow the lawsuit — they need to do one thing the earlier cases didn't demand quite as sharply: verify, in writing, who holds their system before authorizing anything.

Your panels, as always, almost certainly still work. Nothing about a wind-down, an asset sale, or a lawsuit switches off a functioning system. What changes is support: monitoring access, service responsibility, and who answers the phone. That's been true in every case we've covered, and it's true here.

If your installer was PosiGen — or any lease/PPA company

  1. Find your agreement and read page one. Lease or PPA, the document names the system owner and the service contact. After the bankruptcy sale, notices about your account may have come from a company you've never heard of — that letter matters. Keep it.
  2. Confirm who services your system today, in writing. If statements or letters have changed names, the newest one from the asset buyer or servicer is the one that counts. If two companies both claim your account, don't guess — ask each for the document that says so.
  3. Don't sign a "takeover" offer at the door. After every shutdown we've covered, opportunists follow. A legitimate servicer transition comes with paperwork you can read, keep, and verify — never a signature demanded on a doorstep deadline.
  4. Keep paying what your agreement requires unless the company named in your paperwork tells you otherwise in writing. A bankruptcy somewhere in the chain doesn't cancel a lease or PPA, and stopping payments hurts the homeowner, not the defunct installer.
  5. Get your monitoring access sorted. If your app still shows the old company's name — or shows nothing — the monitoring transfer process we describe in our main orphan guide applies here too, and if the alerts themselves are a mystery, our monitoring alerts decoder translates them.

If you own your system outright or through a loan, most of this chapter isn't about you — your situation is the one in the main guide: manufacturer warranties generally survive, the workmanship warranty generally doesn't, and any qualified service company can help you.

The scams that follow a shutdown haven't changed

The red flags from our earlier guides bear repeating, because every wind-down is followed by the same wave:

  • Anyone demanding urgent payment to "keep your warranty active" or "avoid losing your solar credits." Legitimate transfers don't run on a countdown timer.
  • Door-knockers who can't name your system's servicer but are certain you need a new contract today.
  • Offers to "take over" your lease or PPA without paperwork you can read, keep, and verify.
  • Anyone willing to work on your system before looking at a single document. After a shutdown — especially this one — a reputable company verifies first.
