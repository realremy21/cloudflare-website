Denver Votes on Xcel's Next 20 Years Tomorrow — Here's What It Means for Your Solar

By Jeremy & MeralPublished November 2, 2026

If you live in Denver, Broomfield, or Glendale, there's a utility question on your ballot this Tuesday, November 3 — and a lot of money has been spent trying to get your attention on it.

In Denver it's called Referred Question 2K. It asks whether the city should approve a new 20-year franchise agreement with Xcel Energy, starting January 1, 2027. We're not here to tell you how to vote. We're here to do what we always do: sort out what this actually does and doesn't change for a home with solar on the roof.

What a franchise agreement actually is

A franchise agreement is the city's contract that lets Xcel use public streets, alleys, and rights-of-way to deliver electricity and gas. In return, Xcel pays the city for that access — about 34 million dollars a year in franchise fees, according to Colorado Public Radio's reporting on November 1. That fee doesn't come out of thin air: it's collected from customers, on customer bills.

The ballot language itself covers continuing the program to bury overhead power lines, maintaining infrastructure safety, coordinating on emergency management, and Xcel continuing to pay the city for use of public property.

Denver's current agreement expires at the end of this year. Broomfield's and Glendale's run through the end of 2027.

The part worth knowing: the campaign is on your bill, too

CPR reported that Xcel has spent about 4 million dollars on the "Yes on 2K" campaign — and that, per Xcel's own spokesperson, the company plans to recover that campaign cost through the franchise fee over the next 20 years. In other words, whether you vote yes or no, Denver customers are set to pay for the campaign that asked for a yes.

The agreement also includes a one-time 2.5 million dollar contribution for utility payment assistance, plus up to 125,000 dollars a year for low-income energy assistance or conservation. CPR notes Xcel told them even part of that assistance funding comes from both company and customer dollars.

What this vote does NOT change for solar homeowners

This is the part that matters most for our customers, so we'll say it plainly:

  • It does not set your electric rates. Rates are set through the Colorado Public Utilities Commission, not in a city franchise vote.
  • It does not decide whether Xcel serves Denver. If the measure fails, Xcel keeps providing power, the city and Xcel go back to negotiating, and a revised agreement would likely come back to voters — the city itself has said it could return in an April or June election.
  • It does not change net metering, solar incentives, or your interconnection. Nothing in the ballot text touches how your panels are credited for the power they send to the grid.

Your solar keeps working exactly the same either way. Your bill's franchise fee line, your service, and your panels are not on the ballot — the city's 20-year contract terms are.

What a yes vote would lock in, per the agreement

Supporters point to requirements that Xcel keep burying overhead lines (the campaign cites 10 million dollars a year dedicated to undergrounding), maintain Denver's streetlights, coordinate on wildfire and severe-weather preparation, and submit to regular independent audits. Critics — including a former PUC commissioner quoted by CPR — argue a 20-year term with a check-in every five years doesn't give the city enough leverage on things like renewable energy and bill assistance, and they'd rather see a better deal negotiated.

Both of those are worth weighing. That's your call, and it's a real one.

The bottom line for solar homes

Vote Tuesday, November 3 — Denver ballots are due by 7 p.m. Whatever the outcome, nothing about this vote changes how your system produces, how you're credited, or who takes care of your panels. That last part is still us.
