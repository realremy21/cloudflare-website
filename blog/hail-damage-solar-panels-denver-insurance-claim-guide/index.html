Hail Hit Your Panels? The Colorado Solar Insurance-Claim Guide

By Jeremy & MeralPublished November 16, 2026

The storm takes eleven minutes. It sounds like the roof is being worked over with hammers, the dog is under the couch, and then it's sunny again and the street is white with ice. Your roof will get a look — the roofers see to that; they'll be on your street by Friday. Your solar panels, meanwhile, get whatever you think to do next, and most owners think of nothing at all until the production bill arrives or a panel quietly stops pulling its weight.

We're Jeremy and Meral Runnels, the husband-and-wife team behind Mile High Solar Care here in Aurora, and storm documentation is a real part of what we do. This is the guide we wish every Colorado solar owner had the week after a hail storm: what hail actually does to panels (and what it usually doesn't), what an insurance adjuster needs to see to approve a solar claim, and how to build that file before the trail goes cold.

First, the reassuring part: panels are tougher than the storm sounds

Modern solar modules are built and tested for hail. Panels are rated to withstand hailstones around an inch in diameter at highway speeds, and the tempered glass on a quality module shrugs off the pea-to-marble hail that makes up most Colorado storms. Plenty of Front Range arrays come through a loud storm with nothing but a story.

But “rated for hail” is not “immune to hail,” and Colorado is not most places. We sit in hail alley. The same corridor that produces our May-through-September storm season produces golf-ball and occasionally tennis-ball hail, and at that size the math changes:

  • Cracked glass. The obvious one — visible fractures, sometimes in a star or web pattern around the impact.
  • Microcracks you can't see. The sneaky one. An impact that leaves the glass looking intact can fracture the cells underneath. The panel keeps producing — less, and sometimes less every month as the cracks propagate with heat cycles. From the ground, and even from the roof without knowing what to look for, this damage is invisible.
  • Collateral damage. Bent frames, shifted mounts, damaged conduit and wiring, debris driven under the array — the storm doesn't only hit glass.

This is why “the panels look fine from the driveway” is not a damage assessment, and why the claim process below starts with evidence instead of impressions.

Does homeowners insurance cover hail damage to solar panels?

For most Colorado homeowners with rooftop solar they own, generally yes — rooftop panels are typically treated as part of the dwelling structure under a homeowners policy, and hail is a covered peril on standard policies here. That's the general shape of it; your policy is the authority, not this article, and two situations differ:

  • Leased systems and PPAs. If a solar company owns the panels on your roof, the claim and the repair are usually their problem — your job is to report the storm and the production change to them promptly, in writing.
  • Ground mounts and detached structures may sit under different policy sections with different limits.

Either way, the thing that decides claims is not the policy language. It's the documentation. Which brings us to the part roofers understand and solar owners usually don't.

What an adjuster actually needs to approve a solar claim

Adjusters don't deny solar claims out of skepticism; they deny them — or let them die — for lack of a file. A solar claim that gets approved has most of these in it:

  • A dated storm. The claim ties damage to a specific storm event on a specific date. Your insurer will check it against weather records; a claim filed months later with “sometime this summer” starts uphill.
  • Panel-by-panel photos. Not one wide shot of the roof — dated, close documentation of each affected panel, plus wide shots showing the array as a whole. Cracked glass photographs well; suspected microcrack panels get identified by position so they can be tested.
  • Production evidence. Your monitoring app is a witness. A production drop that begins on the storm date — especially one or two panels or a string stepping down while the rest hold — is exactly the pattern adjusters and manufacturers recognize. Screenshot your app's daily production for the weeks before and after the storm before the data scrolls away.
  • A written inspection report from a solar professional. This is the piece homeowners almost never have and adjusters most rely on. Roofers document roofs. A solar inspection documents the system: glass condition per panel, mounting, wiring, critter and debris findings, and an itemized statement of what's damaged and what repair or replacement involves.
  • Repair or replacement estimates tied to the findings, so the claim has numbers attached to evidence.

Build that file and the conversation with your insurer changes completely — you're not asking them to believe a storm hurt your panels; you're showing them.

What to do in the first week after a storm

  1. Note the date and rough time of the storm. Write it down now, not when you file.
  2. Screenshot your monitoring app — daily production for a few weeks before the storm and every day after — and keep an eye out for a step-down pattern.
  3. Don't climb up to inspect it yourself. Wet roofs, damaged glass, and live electrical equipment are a bad combination, and your photos from a ladder won't be the ones the adjuster needs anyway.
  4. Get a solar inspection scheduled and the documentation built while the storm date is fresh and verifiable.
  5. Then call your insurer — with the file in hand, or at minimum with the storm date, the production evidence, and the inspection booked. If a roofer is already filing a roof claim from the same storm, the solar claim rides the same event; make sure the panels are in it as their own documented line, not an afterthought.
  6. Be careful with the door-knockers. After every big storm, out-of-state storm chasers work Denver neighborhoods offering “free solar inspections” that are really sales calls for replacement systems. Your claim needs documentation, not a pitch.

Mistakes that quietly kill solar claims

  • Waiting a season to look. Hail damage discovered in November from a June storm is a causation argument you didn't need to have.
  • Letting the monitoring data scroll away. App history is evidence; preserve it early.
  • Accepting a roof-only settlement that never documented the array, then discovering a dead string later.
  • Assuming “looks fine” means fine. Microcracks don't announce themselves. The production graph and a real inspection are how you know.
  • Cleaning or repairing first, documenting later. Photos before anything changes — always. (This is also why our winter and storm guide gives the same advice about snow season: document before you touch.)

The short version

Colorado hail usually doesn't hurt solar panels — until the storm that does. When it does, claims are won on documentation: a dated storm, panel-by-panel photos, production evidence, and a written solar inspection report. Build that file in the first week and your insurer has everything they need to say yes.

FAQ

Can hail damage solar panels without cracking the glass?

Yes. Impacts can fracture the solar cells under intact glass — microcracks — which reduce that panel's output and can worsen over time with daily heating and cooling. It's the most-missed form of hail damage because nothing looks broken; a documented inspection plus a production drop in your monitoring app is how it gets found and proven.

Should I file a claim before or after getting an inspection?

Get the documentation started first, or at minimum preserve the evidence (storm date, monitoring screenshots) immediately and book the inspection before you file. A claim that arrives with a written solar inspection report and panel-by-panel photos is far easier for an adjuster to approve than a claim that arrives as a phone call and gets documented later.

My roof is being replaced after the storm. Does that cover the panels?

The roof claim covers the roof. Panels usually need to be detached and reset for a re-roof — that's separate solar work — and any hail damage to the panels themselves needs its own documentation and line in the claim. Make sure whoever coordinates your roof work knows the array has been inspected and documented separately.

How long do I have to file a hail claim in Colorado?

Policy deadlines vary — many homeowners policies require claims within a set window after the loss, commonly a year, but your policy controls. The practical deadline is sooner: storm verification, monitoring history, and credible causation all get weaker with time. Treat the first week as the documentation window and don't let a real claim drift.
