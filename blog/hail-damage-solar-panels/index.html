What Hail Does to Solar Panels — and What to Do After the Storm

By Jeremy & MeralPublished September 28, 2026

If you own a home in Colorado, you already know: hail season doesn't mess around. Golf-ball hail, baseball hail — we've all watched it hammer cars, roofs, and skylights. But there's something else up on your roof taking the beating that you might not think about until the damage is done: your solar panels.

Your panels are built tough. They're lab-tested to withstand 1-inch hailstones at highway speeds. But Colorado hail is a different animal — when 2-inch stones start falling, that test rating stops meaning much. After a serious storm, assuming your system is fine because the lights are still on is one of the most expensive assumptions a homeowner can make.

What hail actually does to a solar panel

When a hailstone strikes a panel, the damage usually starts at the glass. A direct hit can shatter the tempered glass outright — sometimes in a clean circular impact point with fractures spidering out across the whole panel, like the photo above. That's the dramatic version, and it's easy to spot.

The sneakier version is what you can't see from the ground: microcracks in the solar cells beneath the glass. The panel looks intact. It still produces power. But those hairline fractures grow over time with heat cycling, and months later you're looking at hot spots, dead cell strings, and production that's quietly slipped well below where it should be.

Here's what most homeowners don't realize: a shattered panel can keep generating electricity. The glass is broken, moisture is getting in, the cells are compromised — and the system hums along, producing less every month while the damage compounds. By the time the production drop is obvious on your monitoring app, you've been losing money for a season.

What to do after a hailstorm

If a serious storm just rolled through, here's the playbook — in order:

1. Stay off the roof — and don't touch damaged panels or exposed wiring. Wet shingles, broken glass, live DC current, and a steep pitch are a bad combination. Everything in the next steps happens from the ground or from a professional's ladder.

2. Do a ground-level visual check. Binoculars help. You're looking for the obvious: shattered or spiderwebbed glass, panels that look visibly displaced, debris sitting on the array.

3. Note the storm details. Date, time, approximate hail size if you saw it. If your neighbors are filing roof claims, that's relevant context. Write it down while it's fresh.

4. Check your production. Open your monitoring app and compare the last few days against the same period last month. A sharp, unexplained dip after a storm date is a red flag — even if everything looks fine from the ground.

5. Get a professional storm documentation visit. This is the step most people skip, and it's the one that matters most. A trained eye on the roof catches the microcracking, seal damage, and mounting issues you'd never see from the yard — and the documentation from that visit is what stands between you and a denied insurance claim.

Why documentation matters more than the repair

Here's the part nobody tells you at the solar sales presentation: your homeowner's insurance may cover hail damage to your panels, but "may" does a lot of heavy lifting. Insurers want dated, detailed evidence — photos of every panel, notes on the damage type, a professional assessment tying it to a specific storm event.

We've seen claims get sideways because the homeowner waited six months, couldn't pin the damage to a date, and had nothing but a blurry phone photo from the driveway. Most policies also expect timely notice, so document and file while the storm date is fresh — a record made the week of the storm carries far more weight than one assembled months later. A proper storm documentation visit gives you a timestamped, panel-by-panel record — real leverage with your insurer. (Hail damage is typically an insurance matter rather than a manufacturer-warranty matter; the same record also covers you if an installer-workmanship question ever arises.) Whether you end up filing or not, that record is yours.

One caveat: if your panels are leased or under a power purchase agreement, the lease company usually owns the maintenance and insurance conversation — call them first, and bring your documentation with you.
