Plug-In Solar in Colorado: What Changes January 1, 2027

By Jeremy & MeralPublished November 27, 2026

We've been Colorado's plain-English source on plug-in solar since before it was a market: we explained the law when it passed, we testified at the Colorado PUC on the meter collar rules at the heart of its rollout, and when Solar Access Colorado launched to help renters use it, we wrote the honest version of that too. This is the next piece — what actually changes on January 1, 2027, and what it means if you're thinking about buying a kit.

We're Jeremy and Meral Runnels, the husband-and-wife team behind Mile High Solar Care in Aurora. We don't sell rooftop systems — we clean, inspect, and maintain solar for Colorado homes, and we now set up plug-in kits too: we stock a small number of vetted kits for customers who want it handled end to end, and if you buy your own kit, we'll set it up for you. Either way, the advice below is the same advice we've given all along.

What the law already allows

Colorado's plug-in solar law (HB 26-1007) covers what it calls a "portable-scale solar generation device": one system per address, capped at 1,920 watts sent to the grid, labeled and listed by a nationally recognized testing laboratory. Your utility can't require approval first, can't charge you a fee for having one, and can't require extra equipment — though it can ask you to let it know you have one and how big it is. The device has to shut itself off during a power outage, which protects the line workers fixing the lines. Small means small: the biggest systems need a licensed electrician and a dedicated circuit, while the smallest tier — 391 watts and under — skips the wiring requirements entirely.

What changes January 1, 2027

Two things, and both are good news for buyers when you understand them:

  • Unlisted bigger kits can no longer be sold here. Starting January 1, selling, leasing, or renting a plug-in device over 391 watts in Colorado without that UL listing is flat-out prohibited. Nothing is taken away from anyone who already owns a kit — the rule governs sales, and its point is simple: the bigger kits move enough power that the state wants an independent lab to have checked them first.
  • Nobody can unreasonably ban your device. The same day, the ban on unreasonable restrictions — including from HOAs, which have to treat these devices as energy-efficiency measures — goes live. Reasonable rules still stand: secure mounting, fire prevention, electrical safety, and notice to your HOA or landlord are all still fair game. A kit dangling off a balcony railing is not what this law protects. But a flat "no" is no longer enforceable.

Before you buy anything, check the label

This is the same thing we said in September, and it matters more now: check the certification before you buy. Look for the listing mark on the product itself and in the listing paperwork — for these devices you'll most often see UL 3700, the safety standard written for exactly these systems. The mark should name the testing laboratory, not just say "tested" or "certified" in the seller's own words. If a seller can't show you the certification, that's your answer.

Questions worth asking any seller

  • Is this exact model labeled and listed — and can you show me the listing, not just a logo on the box?
  • How many watts does it actually send to the grid, and does it shut off automatically during an outage?
  • What does it plug into, and does my home need a dedicated circuit for it?
  • If I move, or my HOA asks questions, what paperwork do I get?

And check your own situation before money moves: your lease, your HOA's actual rules, and whether your balcony or patio gets sun worth harvesting. A north-facing balcony in the shade is a bad investment at any price.

One honest note from us

Plug-in kits and rooftop solar are different doors into the same house. A kit is a real offset for daytime use — it is not a whole-home power plant, and anyone who sells it as one is telling you something. A rooftop system is a long-term asset that needs looking after: kept clean, inspected, and watched for critter damage and wear. We care for both — our plug-in and balcony solar planning covers kit setup, HOA and landlord packets, and product-document review — and we'll keep explaining both in plain English as the rules land, including the PUC's meter collar rules, due by December 31, 2026.

If you're weighing a plug-in kit and want a second set of eyes before you buy — or you already have panels that haven't been cleaned or checked in a while — reach out. That's kind of our whole thing.
