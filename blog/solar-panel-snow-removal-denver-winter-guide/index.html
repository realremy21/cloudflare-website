Winter Solar in Colorado: Snow on Your Panels — What to Do, What NOT to Do, and When to Call Someone

By Jeremy & MeralPublished November 9, 2026

The first real snow of the season lands overnight, you wake up to a white roof, and your solar app shows a flat line. So you stand in the driveway in your slippers, staring up at a buried array, wondering two things: is this hurting anything, and should you be up there with a broom?

We're Jeremy and Meral Runnels, the husband-and-wife team behind Mile High Solar Care here in Aurora, and we get this call after every Front Range storm. Here's the honest version — when snow on your panels actually matters, when Colorado weather fixes it for free, the things you should never do to a snow-covered panel, and the few situations where it's genuinely worth calling someone like us.

Does snow on your panels actually matter?

Yes — but usually for less time, and less money, than people fear.

A panel covered in snow can't see the sun, and a panel that can't see the sun produces nothing. That's the whole problem, and it's temporary. A light dusting lets some light through and melts fast. A full blanket after a real storm takes production to zero until it clears. Partial cover is the sneaky one: a band of snow sitting along the bottom edge of an array can drag production down far more than the covered area suggests, because the cells in a panel — and panels in a string — are wired in series. One buried row can hold back the whole string.

Here's the context that keeps it in proportion. December and January are your lowest-production months anyway, snow or not — short days, low sun angle. Winter losses were never going to be summer losses. And cold itself is not the enemy: solar panels actually run more efficiently in cold weather than in heat. A clear, cold, sunny Colorado day in January is a genuinely good production day once the glass is clear. Studies of snow-belt systems put total annual production lost to snow in the low single digits — real money, but not the emergency it feels like from the driveway.

One more reassurance: your panels are built for this. Modern modules are rated for heavy snow loads — far more than a Denver rooftop storm delivers. Snow sitting on the glass is a production problem, not a structural one.

The good news: Colorado roofs usually clear themselves

If your panels are in Seattle, this article would be longer. On the Front Range, the weather does most of the work:

  • Your panels are tilted, dark, and smooth. Even weak winter sun warms the glass, a thin melt layer forms between the snow and the panel, and the whole blanket tends to let go and slide off in sheets — often within a day of the sun coming out, sometimes in hours.
  • Colorado sun is strong medicine. The same high-altitude sun that gives us 60-degree afternoons two days after a storm works on your array the whole time. Denver's snowiest month is March, and March sun clears panels fast.
  • Wind helps. Front Range wind scours exposed arrays and keeps light snow from ever really setting up.

The typical pattern after a Denver storm: a day of zero or low production, then a slide, then you're back — often while the rest of the roof is still white. If that's your pattern, the correct action was the one you took: none.

When does that pattern break? Flat or low-tilt arrays shed slowly. North-facing sections hold snow longer. And the real troublemaker is a wet storm followed by a hard freeze — the snow melts a little in the afternoon, refreezes overnight, and bonds to the glass as ice. Bonded ice doesn't slide. It also doesn't need to be attacked, which brings us to the important part.

What NOT to do to a snow-covered panel

This is the section that saves people money. Every winter, panels get damaged by owners who were only trying to help. Module manufacturers — Jinko and LONGi say it in so many words in their maintenance manuals — prohibit most of the obvious approaches, and damage from improper cleaning can void your panel warranty.

  • No metal tools on the glass. Ever. Metal shovels, ice scrapers, and metal-edged roof rakes scratch and crack panel glass. Those scratches are permanent, they cost you production forever, and they can kill a warranty claim.
  • No hot water. Pouring hot water on a frozen panel is a thermal-shock experiment. Tempered glass that goes from below freezing to hot in seconds can crack. Even if it doesn't crack this time, you've stressed it.
  • Never force bonded ice. If snow or ice is frozen to the glass, prying, chiseling, or hammering at it is exactly what the manufacturers prohibit. Ice that's bonded will release on its own when the weather turns. Ice that's forced takes glass and cell material with it.
  • No pressure washers. Not for snow, not for grime. High-pressure water damages seals and can drive moisture where it doesn't belong.
  • Never walk on your panels. Snow or no snow. Panels are not a walking surface — you'll crack cells you can't see and create damage that shows up months later.
  • Don't climb a snowy or icy roof at all. This one isn't about the panels. A winter roof is how homeowners get hurt. Nothing your array produces in a week is worth a fall.

If a neighbor, a handyman, or a video suggests any of the above, that's a sign to stop, not a technique to copy.

What you can safely do from the ground

Most of the time: nothing, patiently. If you want to help a slow shed along and your setup allows it, there is a safe version:

  • Soft tools only — a foam-head snow rake or a soft-bristle brush, the same tool classes the manufacturers approve for cleaning. Gentle pressure, loose snow only, and stop the moment you feel resistance. Resistance means ice, and ice means stop.
  • From the ground or a stable ladder position — never standing on the snowy roof itself. If the array isn't safely reachable this way, it isn't a DIY job.
  • Work the bottom edge first. Clearing the lower band of panels frees the slide path, and the rest often follows on its own.
  • A leaf blower is fine for one thing: light, dry powder that hasn't compacted — a tool LONGi's manual lists as acceptable. Against wet or refrozen snow it does nothing, and forcing the issue isn't an option.

And if you do nothing? That's also fine. Waiting is a legitimate strategy, and on most Colorado roofs it's the winning one.

When snow becomes a call-someone problem

Here's when "wait for the sun" stops being the right answer:

  • Snow or ice is still sitting there after several sunny days while your neighbors' arrays have cleared. Something about your tilt, shading, or the ice bond needs a closer look.
  • Your roof is clear but production is still at or near zero. Now it's probably not a snow problem at all — think a tripped breaker, an inverter fault, or a string that's down. That's a diagnostic, not a broom.
  • You can see something wrong: a panel that looks cracked or fogged, anything bent or shifted, debris piled against the array, or icicles forming at the array's bottom edge storm after storm (a sign water is being trapped where it shouldn't be).
  • The array is ground-mounted or on a business, and downtime is money. Commercial arrays don't get the "just wait" advice a house does — a big flat field doesn't shed like a pitched roof, and lost production is a line item. Professional snow clearing with soft-surface tools exists for exactly this case.

Before winter: the two things that actually prevent problems

Almost everything bad that happens to a Front Range array in winter sneaks in before the snow does.

Get critter guards on before the cold sets in. Winter is when pigeons and squirrels most want the sheltered space under your panels — it's warm, it's dry, and it's protected. A nest packed under an array traps moisture and snowmelt against your roof, and the chewed wiring we find in spring often starts as a winter tenant. A proper critter guard closes that space off for good. After the storm: read your monitoring like a pro

Your Enphase or SolarEdge app is the difference between knowing and guessing. After a storm, use it like this:

  1. Give it a day. Check production the first full sunny day after the snow has visibly cleared. A zero during the storm is weather. A zero after a clear day is information.
  2. Look at the array map, not just the total. Modern systems show panel-level or string-level production. If most panels are producing and one group is flat, that group has a problem — shading from a snow drift is possible, but so is a downed string, and only one of those fixes itself.
  3. Compare against a similar day, not against July. A clear January day producing half of a clear June day is normal. A clear January day producing half of last January's clear days is a signal.
  4. Check after every big storm, the same way. Two minutes in the app after each storm builds the habit that catches the one problem in ten that doesn't fix itself — while it's still small, and while storm documentation is still fresh if you ever need it for a claim.

FAQ

Should I pay for solar panel snow removal at my home in Denver?

Usually, no. On a pitched residential roof, Colorado sun and tilt clear most snow within a day or two, and the production you'd recover rarely covers a service call. Snow removal earns its money on flat, low-tilt, ground-mounted, and commercial arrays — and on residential roofs in the specific problem cases listed above. Anyone who tells every Denver homeowner they need routine snow removal is selling, not advising.

Can snow damage my solar panels?

The snow itself, almost never — panels are load-rated well beyond what our storms deliver. The damage we actually see comes from removal attempts: metal tools, forced ice, pressure washers, and people walking on panels. The other winter damage path is old hail cracks meeting freeze-thaw, which is a pre-winter inspection problem, not a snow problem.

My panels are producing nothing two days after the storm. What now?

First, look: if they're still covered, it's still weather — give the sun a chance. If the glass is clear and the app still shows zero, check your breaker and your inverter's status lights, then call someone. A clear array producing nothing is electrical until proven otherwise.

Will clearing snow void my warranty?

Clearing it wrong can. Manufacturer manuals approve soft brushes, foam tools, and similar gentle methods, and prohibit forcing frozen snow and ice off the glass. Damage caused by improper cleaning methods is exactly the kind of thing warranty claims get denied over. When in doubt: soft tools, loose snow, no force — or leave it alone.

The short version

Snow on your panels costs you production, not panels — and on most Denver-area roofs, a day or two of sun fixes it for free. The expensive mistakes are all impatience: metal tools, hot water, forced ice, and icy roof climbs. Keep critters out before winter, get hail damage documented before freeze-thaw finds it, and check your app the first clear day after every storm. If the snow won't shed, the ice won't quit, or the app says zero on a clear day, that's when you call.
