We Went to the PUC to Speak for Colorado Homeowners: The Meter Collar Hearing, Recapped

By Jeremy & MeralPublished November 9, 2026

On Monday, November 9, we spent the afternoon at a Colorado Public Utilities Commission hearing — more than forty people on the call: utilities, manufacturers, trade groups, county commissioners, and us. We're Jeremy and Meral Runnels, the husband-and-wife team behind Mile High Solar Care in Aurora. The hearing (proceeding 26R-0356E) was about the rules for meter collar adapters — the devices at the center of Colorado's new plug-in solar law — and we were there for one reason: we were the only people in the room who work at homeowners' meters every week and had nothing to sell.

We don't sell meter collars, and we don't do meter work. We clean, inspect, and maintain people's solar, and that work happens right at the meter, where these adapters live.

First, what is a meter collar?

It's a small adapter that sits between your electric meter and its socket and gives a solar system a safe, certified place to connect — no main panel upgrade, no rewiring project. Colorado's plug-in solar law (HB 26-1007) says these devices belong in the state's solar future, and it told the PUC to write the approval rules by the end of this year. Monday's hearing was the public's turn to speak on what those rules should say.

The story that hung over the hearing

A few years ago, a certified device — Tesla's Backup Switch — was approved here and installed in Colorado homes. This past August, its approval was pulled. The utility's own in-house heat testing was the basis; the testing method was never disclosed, and Tesla told the Commission the device has been installed in tens of thousands of homes nationally without incident, including in Arizona heat. An installer who spoke Monday described getting the news by email with eleven days' notice, then asking what standard the device had failed — and never getting a meaningful answer. The workaround costs a homeowner roughly $3,000 to $8,000 more than the collar would have, and storage projects stretch out four months or longer.

Here's how we put it to the judge, in our own testimony:

"When a list changes in silence, the question lands on the homeowner — and on companies like mine, standing at their meter."

What we asked for

Three simple things, all of them paperwork, none of them asking anyone to approve anything unsafe:

  1. Start the clock. When a complete application goes in, the ninety-day review clock actually starts — and the utility says in writing within fifteen days if something is missing.
  2. Publish the list. The approved list is public, dated, and names each device by maker, model, and certification, so a crew can check it on a phone, standing at a meter.
  3. Say what happens when a device comes off. If a device is removed, the notice states when that takes effect and what happens to the ones already installed in people's homes.

And we meant this part, too:

"We're not asking you to approve anything unsafe. Safety comes first — our crews work around this equipment, and we want the utility's safety judgment to hold."

What the rest of the room showed

The solar industry's joint case — presented by the Colorado Solar and Storage Association and the Solar Energy Industries Association — was that safe should mean meeting national standards, the same kind of independent certification the utilities already accept for meter sockets and inverters. Their exhibits pointed out that today, the largest utility's permanent approved list holds zero collars, that its own manual names minimum safety requirements but no actual test, procedure, or pass-fail line, and that forty states plus D.C. already approve at least one collar. A meter collar maker's engineers walked through the standard heat testing these devices pass, and a Colorado master electrician noted that under the utility's own manual, the meter housing is the customer's property — a collar is an extension of that housing, installed by licensed contractors and inspected like other customer equipment.

The utilities asked to keep their discretion. One cooperative called collars a black box and said a national listing is a floor, not proof a device is safe on every system. Black Hills argued that safety responsibility ultimately lands on the utility, so utilities need room to judge. Rural electric cooperatives raised self-governance. Those are real concerns, and the judge pressed both sides on them for most of the afternoon — including a long stretch on whether testing requirements belong in formal tariffs where they can be challenged, or in utility manuals where they can't.

Local voices leaned toward access: a consumer advocacy group, commissioners from Clear Creek and Gilpin counties (one described five-day wildfire-safety shutoffs costing people refrigerated medicine, food, and heat), and an Erie town council member speaking for lower-income homeowners. That's the side of the room we were proud to sit on.

What happens next

No decision was made Monday, and we won't pretend otherwise. The judge said there is no comment deadline — he will read everything filed in the proceeding right up until he issues his recommended decision, which he expects to take the next couple of weeks, maybe longer. Then the Commission itself decides the final rules. The law sets the hard dates: rules adopted by December 31, 2026, and plug-in solar goes live in Colorado on January 1, 2027.

Your voice still counts

The record is still open. If you own solar, want plug-in solar, or just believe the rules for your own meter should be written down where you can read them, you can file a comment in proceeding 26R-0356E through the Commission's e-filing system or the comment form at puc.colorado.gov. It takes a few minutes, and every comment becomes part of the record the decision is built on.

We closed our testimony the way we'd close this post:

"These devices are a doorway. For a lot of Colorado families — smaller homes, tighter budgets — they're how solar becomes possible at all. Let's keep that doorway safe, and let's make it easier to walk through: more accessible, less red tape, rules written down where everyone can see them."
