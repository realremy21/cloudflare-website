Your Solar App Is Trying to Tell You Something: Monitoring Alerts, Decoded

By Jeremy & MeralPublished November 24, 2026

It usually happens at breakfast. Your phone buzzes, you open your solar app, and there's a red banner: "Microinverter not reporting." Or the production graph that was fine last month suddenly drops off a cliff. Or the app just shows yesterday's numbers, frozen, like the system fell asleep.

We're Jeremy and Meral Runnels, the husband-and-wife team behind Mile High Solar Care here in Aurora. Monitoring alerts are one of the most common reasons people call us across the Denver metro — Enphase, SolarEdge, and Tesla systems alike. Some of those alerts mean "drop everything." Most mean "your Wi-Fi hiccuped." The trouble is, the apps don't tell you which is which.

So here's the decoder we walk our own customers through: what each alert actually means, what you can safely ignore, and what deserves a service call.

First, know the difference between a power problem and a data problem

Every monitoring system is a chain: your panels and inverters talk to a gateway box (Enphase calls it the IQ Gateway or Envoy; Tesla and SolarEdge have their own versions), the gateway talks to your home Wi-Fi, and your Wi-Fi talks to the app. A break anywhere in that chain can make the app look like your solar died when the roof is producing fine.

That gives you the single most useful question in solar troubleshooting: did the system stop making power, or did it stop reporting? Almost every alert below is one or the other, and they have completely different urgency. A system that stopped reporting is a data problem. A system that stopped producing is money walking out the door every sunny day it sits there.

"Microinverter not reporting" (Enphase)

This is the alert that sends most people to Google — often typed exactly that way, "Enphase app says microinverter not reporting." On an Enphase system, every panel has its own microinverter, and this alert means one of them hasn't checked in with the gateway.

If it's one panel: you're losing roughly one panel's worth of production. That's real, but it's not an emergency. Causes we actually find on roofs: a microinverter that has genuinely failed, a connection problem under the panel, or wiring damaged by critters nesting under the array — pigeons and squirrels chewing under panels is a Front Range specialty, and we wrote the full guide to it here: Pigeon-Proofing Solar Panels: The Front Range Guide. Occasionally it's a one-off communication glitch that clears on its own within a day.

If it's all of them at once: thirty microinverters did not fail on the same morning. That's the gateway — check whether it has power, whether its breaker tripped, and whether your Wi-Fi changed. Start there, not on the roof.

What to do: look at today's production graph. If the total dropped by about one panel's share and one microinverter is dark, give it a day to clear. If it's still silent after a couple of days, book service — a dead microinverter is usually warranty equipment, and a dated record of when it stopped reporting is exactly what a warranty claim wants.

"Gateway not reporting" — or the app just shows old numbers

When the app freezes on yesterday's data or says the gateway hasn't reported, the system is almost always still producing. The app is blind; the roof isn't. The usual causes are boring: a new router, a changed Wi-Fi password, an internet provider swap, or the gateway's breaker switched off (often it's on a circuit nobody labeled, in the garage or by the main panel).

What to do: check that the gateway is powered and its lights look normal, then reconnect it to your Wi-Fi in the app if it dropped off. When the connection comes back, the missing days usually backfill on their own. Call for help if it's been dark more than about 48 hours and you can't confirm the system is producing any other way — or if it keeps dropping off the network every few weeks, which points to a gateway or placement problem worth fixing properly.

"Why did my solar production drop?"

This is the vaguest worry of all, because half the time nothing is wrong. Before you panic, compare fairly:

  • Season. A Colorado system in late November produces a fraction of what it makes in June — shorter days, lower sun. A "drop" that tracks the calendar is astronomy, not failure.
  • Weather and snow. A cloudy week is a cloudy week. And panels under snow produce close to nothing until it slides or melts off — normal after a Front Range storm, not a malfunction.
  • Dirt. Dust, pollen, wildfire-smoke film, and bird droppings all shave production, gradually. The fix is a cleaning — ours is pure water and a soft brush, never soap or pressure on the glass — at $129 for up to 12 panels, $179 for 13–24, and $249 for 25–40, with a $50 surcharge for two-story homes.

The drop that does deserve a call looks different: a step change. Production was fine Tuesday, it's down a big chunk Wednesday, and it stays there on clear days. That pattern means something physical changed — a string or a component went down, debris or nesting is suddenly shading part of the array, or a new shadow is. Step changes don't fix themselves.

Grid alerts: "Grid outage," "Grid is down," off-grid mode

If your system has a battery — Tesla Powerwall, Enphase IQ Battery, SolarEdge backup — a grid alert during an actual outage is informational. Your system saw the grid fail and did its job. Solar-only systems shut down during outages too, by design: it's a safety rule so your panels never energize a line a utility crew is repairing. That's why panels "don't work" in a blackout without a battery, and it's not a fault.

The version that matters: your lights are on, the neighbors' lights are on, and the app insists the grid has been down for hours. That's usually a sensor or gateway issue. If it clears, forget it. If it sticks around for a day, it's worth a diagnostic visit — a system that can't see the grid correctly will make other bad decisions too.

Consumption numbers that make no sense

Your production can be perfect while your consumption data is fiction: home usage reading negative, doubling overnight, or frozen at one number. Apps flag this as a consumption-meter or CT error, or don't flag it at all — you just notice your "home used" numbers stopped resembling your life.

The usual cause is the current transformers (CTs) — the little clamps that measure your home's usage — installed in the wrong spot, facing the wrong way, or configured wrong at setup. It doesn't stop your system from producing, but every decision you make from that data is built on sand. Fixing it is a hands-on diagnostic job, not an app setting you should have to hunt for.

Battery alerts

Battery notices split cleanly in two. Informational: Storm Watch and storm-mode notices (your battery is holding a full charge ahead of bad weather — that's a feature), firmware update notices, and brief "battery is calibrating" messages. Worth a call: a battery that has stopped charging or discharging entirely, repeated battery error codes, or a battery that quietly drops out of the app and doesn't come back. Battery work is electrical work — on our team it's performed by our licensed electrical contractor partner, with findings in writing either way.

Inverter errors (SolarEdge and other string systems)

Enphase spreads the work across a microinverter per panel, so one failure costs you one panel. A string system runs everything through one central inverter — which makes an inverter error the most urgent alert on this list. If your SolarEdge inverter shows an error code, a red light, or the app reads zero production on a sunny day, the whole system is down, and every day it sits is a full day of production lost. Don't let that one ride for a month. (It's also why we told Enphase owners last week to make sure their monitoring is actually reporting — an error you never see is an error that never gets fixed.)

The 60-second triage: three questions

Before you call anyone — including us — answer these three, right in the app:

  1. Is production actually zero or missing, or just the data? Look at today's production graph, not just the alert banner. A normal-looking curve with a scary banner is usually a data problem.
  2. One panel, or everything? One panel is a component problem — real, schedulable, rarely urgent. Everything at once is a gateway, breaker, or inverter problem — and if production is truly zero, it's urgent.
  3. What changed? Storm last night? Snow on the roof? New router or Wi-Fi password? Breaker work, construction, a tree that grew? Half of all alerts confess right here.

Those three answers sort nearly every alert into ignore, watch, or call. Here's the cheat sheet version:

Safe to ignore or watch: a firmware-update notice; a grid alert during a real outage; low production while panels are under snow; one gateway dropout that reconnects and backfills; a single microinverter that's silent for a day.

Worth a service call: zero production on a sunny day; any inverter error code; the same microinverter silent for more than a few days; consumption data that's been nonsense for weeks; a gateway that keeps falling offline; and any alert that comes with a tripped breaker — if a breaker trips again after one reset, stop resetting it and call. That's the system telling you something electrical is wrong.

The short version

Most monitoring alerts are the app losing track of a healthy system. The ones that matter are a step-change drop, a silent microinverter that stays silent, an inverter error, and zero production on a sunny day. Learn to tell data problems from power problems and you'll never lose a month of production to a banner you were afraid to tap — or a week of worry to one that meant nothing.
