Your Solar Installer Went Bankrupt — Now What?

By Jeremy & MeralPublished October 29, 2026

If the company that installed your solar panels has closed, you're not alone — and your system isn't worthless. You're what's becoming known in the industry as a "solar orphan": a homeowner with a working (or not-working) system on the roof and nobody left to call.

In Colorado, that now includes roughly 5,000 Freedom Forever installations (a Scout Data figure based on permit records), every customer of Denver's EcoMark Solar, which closed in 2022/23, Titan Solar Power customers from before its June 2024 shutdown, and homeowners whose Sunnova or SunPower contracts were transferred to a servicer they've never heard of.

Freedom Forever's path is the most recent: the company filed Chapter 11 on April 15, 2026, and the case converted to Chapter 7 liquidation on August 7, 2026. The bankruptcy claim deadline passed on October 16, 2026. Every Freedom Forever customer is now a service and warranty orphan — there is no company left to honor the workmanship warranty or answer the phone. (Our earlier post covers the Freedom Forever bankruptcy itself and the document-saving steps in detail.)

Here's the practical guide we wish someone handed every orphaned owner.

Step 1: Find out who actually services your loan or lease

Your installer going bankrupt does not cancel your loan or lease — but it often changes who you pay and who you call. Check in this order:

  • Look at page 1 of your financing agreement. The lender or servicer named there is your first call — it's often not the installer at all.
  • Mosaic loans are now serviced by Solar Servicing LLC (866-493-6367, loanservicing@solarservicingllc.com) after Mosaic's June 2025 bankruptcy moved roughly $8B of servicing there.
  • Sunnova and SunPower contracts transferred to SunStrong Management. If your app or statements changed names, that's why.
  • GoodLeap loans: 1-800-345-9372.
  • Freedom Forever customers: your equipment financing (if any) sits with the lender named in your paperwork, separate from the installer. Your installer's bankruptcy doesn't pause those payments — talk to the servicer before changing anything.

If you can't find your paperwork, your monthly bank statement showing the payment usually names the servicer. Start there.

Step 2: Understand what your warranty actually covers now

There are two different warranties, and bankruptcy treats them very differently:

  • Manufacturer equipment warranties (your panels, inverter, batteries) generally survive your installer's closure. Enphase and SolarEdge both state that eligible components remain covered when an installer closes. These warranties usually cover the part — diagnosis, labor, removal and reinstallation may or may not be included, so read the terms for your specific equipment.
  • Installer workmanship warranties and production guarantees were promises from the company that closed. With Freedom Forever in Chapter 7 liquidation, there is no company left to honor them. EcoMark customers learned this the hard way — one Denver homeowner only discovered EcoMark had shut down when their panels stopped working and the "lifetime warranty" turned out to have no one behind it.

Practical move: register or confirm your equipment directly with the manufacturer now, while it's easy, and save every document somewhere you control — not in a customer portal that could go dark.

Step 3: Check whether your system is even producing

The most common story we hear from orphaned owners isn't a dramatic failure — it's silence. The system stopped producing weeks or months ago and nobody noticed, because the company that used to watch it is gone. Meanwhile the loan or lease payment keeps coming out, and the utility bill climbs back to full price. Reviewers describe it exactly that way: one homeowner reported a "$500 utility bill on top of my standard $370 monthly solar payment."

Three checks you can do today:

  1. Open your monitoring app (Enphase, SolarEdge, Tesla, etc.). Look at today's production and the last 30 days. Flat zero, or a cliff where production suddenly dropped and never recovered, means something is wrong.
  2. Compare your utility bill to the same month last year. If usage from the grid jumped while your habits didn't, your system is likely underproducing or down.
  3. Look at the inverter. A red light, an error code, or a blank screen is information — photograph it. A green/normal display with zero app production often means a monitoring/communications problem, not a dead system — a much cheaper fix.

Stay off the roof, and don't open electrical equipment. These checks are all ground-level and app-level.

The 5 failure modes that show up at orphan-system age

Most orphaned systems in Colorado are now 3–10 years old — exactly the age when these appear:

  1. Critter damage. Birds nesting under panels and squirrels chewing wiring. In Colorado this is the single most common physical problem we find on unmaintained arrays — and it gets worse every season it's ignored.
  2. Dirty panels. Dust, pollen, and bird droppings build up. A visibly dirty array is losing production you already paid for.
  3. Monitoring gone dark. The cellular plan expired, the gateway lost connection, or the account was tied to the installer. The system may be fine — but you're flying blind.
  4. Inverter trouble. Inverters are the hardest-working component and the most common electrical failure point at this age. One reviewer had an inverter dead for over a month in peak summer while their installer didn't fix it.
  5. Nobody watching. No alerts, no annual check, no one who notices a string is down. Small problems become expensive ones quietly.

When cleaning or critter guard is the fix — and when you need an electrician

Honest sorting matters here, because orphaned owners get sold things they don't need.

Maintenance fixes it: dirty panels, bird nesting, missing or failed critter guard, monitoring reconnection help, and a documented health check that tells you what shape everything is in.

You need a licensed electrician or the manufacturer: a dead inverter, damaged wiring, breaker or electrical faults, and anything involving opening electrical equipment. We don't do electrical repairs, and any company offering to "take over your warranty" should be able to show you, in writing, who actually stands behind it.

You may just need information: if your system is producing normally, the best move might be nothing more than confirming your servicer, registering your equipment, and having a local number to call next time.
