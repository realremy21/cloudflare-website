Colorado Has a New Governor Coming. For Solar Homes, the Real Story Is the PUC.

By Jeremy & MeralPublished November 6, 2026

Colorado voters chose a new governor on Tuesday: Phil Weiser, the state's attorney general, who won decisively and will take office in January, succeeding Jared Polis. Congratulations and politics aside, we want to walk you through the one part of this result that will actually touch your roof and your electric bill over the next four years — because it isn't the governor's office itself. It's a three-person board most homeowners have never heard of.

Meet the PUC — the board that sets your solar rules

The Colorado Public Utilities Commission is three commissioners, appointed by the governor and confirmed by the state Senate. That small board approves or rejects Xcel Energy's rate increases, oversees how solar homes are credited for the power they send to the grid, and signs off on the utility's long-range energy plans. When your bill changes, or the rules for your panels change, it almost always runs through the PUC — not through Congress, and not through a city vote like Denver's Question 2K this week.

Why this election matters for that board

Here's the fact that makes Tuesday's result different: all three PUC seats come open during Governor-elect Weiser's term.

  • Commissioner Tom Plant's term ends in January 2027. (Governor Polis may fill this seat before he leaves office — and by law, no more than two of the three commissioners can be from the same party.)
  • Commissioner Megan Gilman's term ends in January 2028.
  • Chairman Eric Blank's term ends in January 2029.

So by the end of the new governor's term, every person deciding Colorado's utility rates and solar rules will have been appointed under his administration. Governor-elect Weiser campaigned on leaning further into the clean-energy shift — his platform pledges to accelerate solar and wind development across Colorado — and groups watching utility bills closely noted his first stated priority is guardrails so new data centers don't push their power costs onto regular customers. What that means in practice will be decided case by case, in rate cases and rule-makings, over the next four years.

What this does NOT change today

Same as we told you after the Denver vote — an election result is not a rate change:

  • Your rates did not change on Tuesday. No rate case has been filed or decided because of this result.
  • Your net metering and interconnection did not change. The credits your system earns today are the credits it earns tomorrow.
  • No solar incentive came back — yet. There's early talk in Washington about restoring federal solar tax credits, and analysts say the more likely first move is permitting reform to get projects connected faster. As of today, no bill has passed and the expired homeowner credit is still expired. Be wary of anyone who tells you otherwise to sell you a system.
  • You don't need to do anything. No forms, no calls, no changes to your system.

What we'll be watching for you

Three things, in order: who gets nominated for that first PUC seat in early 2027, the next Xcel rate case and how the new commission handles it, and the 2027 legislative session — where the new governor's data-center and clean-energy priorities get their first real test. When any of those turn into something that actually changes your bill or your credits, we'll write it up here the same day, in plain English, like we always do.

The bottom line for solar homes

The governor's mansion is changing hands, and over four years that will reshape the board that sets your solar rules. Nothing about your system, your credits, or your bill changed this week. Your panels' job is unchanged — and taking care of them is still ours.
